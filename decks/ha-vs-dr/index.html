Welcome. In about 20 minutes we'll answer two questions: how do we stay up when something breaks, and how do we get back up when something big breaks?

Those are two different things with two different price tags. By the end you'll know the two numbers that decide how much we should spend on each.

the question nobody likes asking out loud…

“It's in the cloud, so it can't go down… right?”

It can.

“Everything fails, all the time.”

Werner Vogels, Chief Technology Officer, Amazon

The cloud doesn't stop things breaking. It gives us better tools to survive it, if we choose to use them.

Let the room answer first. Most people assume the big providers simply don't go down.

The quote is from Werner Vogels, Amazon's CTO, who has said it in talks for years (for example in his 2016 post "10 Lessons from 10 Years of Amazon Web Services" on his All Things Distributed blog). If the people running one of the biggest clouds plan for failure, so should we.

Disks die, power fails, people make mistakes, software has bugs. The question isn't "if", it's "what happens next".

two ideas that get mixed up all the time

Two different questions

🛩️

High availability · HA

“How do we stay up?”

Like a plane with two engines. Lose one and it keeps flying. Passengers barely notice.

automatic · seconds

🛟

Disaster recovery · DR

“How do we get back up?”

Like lifeboats and a rehearsed drill. The ship is in trouble, but everyone gets to safety and carries on.

a plan · minutes to days

We need both. They protect us from different things.

HA (high availability) is about absorbing everyday failures without anyone noticing: a server dies, its twin takes over.

DR (disaster recovery) is about the big, rare event: a whole site is lost, or our data is damaged. It's a plan, people and rehearsals, not just technology. Backups are one part of DR.

Ask the room: which one do you think we spend more on today?

The provider (hard hat) builds the datacentres and regions. We decide where to put our copies. That's our design choice, and our responsibility.

1) A server dies: our twin copy in another datacentre takes over. That's HA. 2) A whole datacentre goes dark: still fine, because we spread across two. 3) A whole region goes down: HA can't help, because both copies were in that region. Now we need DR: switch to a copy in another region. 4) Ransomware or someone deletes data by mistake: HA faithfully copies the damage to every copy in seconds. Only a backup from before the damage saves us.

"Region" and "availability zone" are the terms AWS, Azure and Google Cloud all use. A zone is one or more datacentres with separate power and networking.

Press → (or click) to move from 99% to 99.999% ("five nines"). The downtime figures are simple arithmetic on 365.25 days a year.

The jump from 99.9% to 99.99% means going from almost nine hours a year to under an hour. That usually means running everything twice in different datacentres. Five nines usually means multiple regions and a lot of automation. The "what it takes" and the cost meter are illustrative rules of thumb, not quotes.

The Azure figures come from Microsoft's published SLA (service level agreement) for Virtual Machines. AWS and Google Cloud publish similar tiers. Remember, an SLA is a refund promise, not a guarantee the system stays up.

when the worst does happen…

Two numbers run the show

RPO

Recovery Point Objective

How much data can we afford to lose?

💾“When did we last press save?”

Measured in time. An RPO of 1 hour means we could lose the last hour of orders, emails or edits.

RTO

Recovery Time Objective

How long can we afford to be down?

⏱️“How long until we can type again?”

An RTO of 4 hours means the system must be back, working, within 4 hours of the disaster.

These are business decisions. IT's job is to price them.

The document analogy works well: RPO is how much typing you lose since your last save. RTO is how long until your laptop works again.

Ask the room: for our main customer system, what would you say? Most people say "zero and zero". The next slide shows why that answer is expensive.

The key message: the business sets these numbers, based on what downtime and lost data actually cost us. IT designs to hit them.

These four patterns are described in the AWS whitepaper "Disaster Recovery of Workloads on AWS: Recovery in the Cloud". Azure and Google Cloud describe the same ideas with slightly different names. "Active/active" is also called "multi-site".

The analogies: flat-pack means everything's in the box but you have to build it. A pilot light is the tiny flame that's always on so the boiler can fire up fast. An understudy is dressed and waiting in the wings. Two kitchens means if one catches fire, diners barely notice.

The "back in" times are typical ranges, not promises. The cost meter is illustrative.

take these back to your teams

Six questions worth asking on Monday

  1. Do our key systems have an agreed RTO and RPO, signed off by the business?
  2. Does what we pay for actually match those numbers?
  3. When did we last test a recovery, end to end, and how long did it take?
  4. Is at least one backup safe from ransomware, so it can't be changed or deleted?
  5. Are our critical systems spread across more than one datacentre?
  6. Who declares a disaster, and how do we reach people if email is down?

None of these need technical knowledge to ask. If a team can't answer one confidently, that's useful information.

A plan that hasn't been tested is a hope. Tests can start small: a "tabletop" walk-through in a meeting room, then restoring one system, then a full failover.

Backups that "can't be changed or deleted" are often called immutable backups. All three big providers offer them.
